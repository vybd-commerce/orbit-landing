Vybd exists to redesign the economics of how products move through the world — bringing the cost of getting a product from the person who makes it to the person who uses it as close to zero as possible, while ensuring that every cost that remains is transparent, justified, and earned.

This isn't necessarily about eliminating every intermediary. Logistics providers, payment processors, distributors, and other participants in the value chain can create real value. It's about challenging the assumption that every layer, markup, and cost is necessary simply because it's always been there. Every participant in the value chain should earn their place.

## Where this started

Before Vybd, I worked on BulkMagic, a marketplace that let consumers pool orders and buy in bulk directly from wholesalers and producers. That was when I was first exposed to the retail industry. In an early interview, I was asked what drove me to build BulkMagic. D2C was booming, and I could see the savings that were possible. My somewhat timid answer: “To dismantle the traditional middleman model, connect producers directly with consumers, and fundamentally reengineer the economics of commerce”. My interviewer looked at me with barely concealed ridicule, as though I'd just suggested something so detached from reality that it wasn't worth taking seriously.

That idea never left me. But several years and a lot of shipments later, I understand why the reaction was fair. The goal was right; my model of the problem was wrong.

## What I got wrong about middlemen

I used to see intermediaries as actors who had grabbed an unfair position in the chain and were taxing everyone else for it. Some are. But most middlemen survive because they do something real that the producer cannot or will not do:

- They carry capital. A distributor buys inventory on credit and waits 60 to 90 days to be paid.
- They carry risk. An importer of record takes on legal liability; a retailer absorbs returns and unsold stock.
- They carry trust. A marketplace or a store lets a buyer pay a brand they have never heard of.
- They carry demand. A buyer relationship or a shelf gets the product in front of people.

You cannot remove a middleman without absorbing what they did. That was what my interviewer understood and I did not.

The second thing I missed: disintermediation almost always re-intermediates. Shopify did not remove the middleman for small brands. It moved the tollbooth from the shelf to the feed. A direct-to-consumer brand on Shopify now pays its toll to Meta, Google, TikTok and Amazon ads, often a large share of revenue. The middleman changed shape; it did not disappear.

So the honest version of my goal is not "cut out the middlemen." It is: strip out the rent, keep the service, and drive the cost of coordination toward zero. That is why the line I keep coming back to is AI is the only middleman we need.

## Where the leverage actually sits

Getting a product from raw material to a consumer is a chain of decisions, and power concentrates wherever one actor controls something the others can't route around. My original map had five blocks. It was missing the two where the most leverage sits: money and demand.

- What to make (market research, positioning). Platforms and agencies with data hold the leverage today; shared, cheap market intelligence breaks it.
- How to make it (design, sourcing, manufacturing). Factories with MOQs and sourcing agents hold it; pooled orders and verified supplier data break it.
- How to move it (freight, customs, compliance, warehousing). Forwarders, brokers and 3PLs with opaque pricing hold it; volume and transparent landed cost break it.
- How to sell it (channels, marketing, buyers). Ad platforms, marketplaces and retail buyers hold it; owned audiences, buyer access and multi-channel reach break it.
- How to fund it (production, inventory, freight, receivables). Distributors, lenders and anyone who can wait to be paid hold it; underwriting from real operating data breaks it.
- How to keep it trusted (liability, returns, payments, reviews). Marketplaces, importers of record and retailers hold it; verifiable records and shared accountability break it.
- How to improve it (customer feedback). Whoever owns the customer relationship holds it; the producer owning their customer data breaks it.

Two rows dominate. Demand is the hardest: if a brand can't acquire customers profitably, nothing else matters. Capital is the quietest: producers lose margin mostly because they can't wait 90 days to get paid, and whoever can wait takes the cut. Any plan that only addresses information leaves both untouched.

## What AI changes, and what it doesn't

I started Vybd because I watched Claude Code change how software gets made, and I was sure the same thing would happen to anyone selling physical products. I still believe that. But the analogy only holds for one layer of the chain.

Software has near-zero marginal cost, instant feedback and no inventory. Physical goods have minimum order quantities, lead times, freight, duties, spoilage, returns, regulation and cash locked in a container for two months.

AI collapses the coordination and knowledge work: market research, compliance paperwork, product data and content, listings, landed-cost math, buyer discovery, partner management, reconciliation. Work that used to need a team of specialists becomes an agent plus a senior operator reviewing it.

AI does not collapse atoms, cash cycles or trust. Goods still have to be made, moved and stored. Someone still has to finance them. Someone still has to be accountable when something goes wrong.

## What Vybd is building

Vybd is building two things that depend on each other:

1. A system that lets any producer reach consumers anywhere, whatever their informational or financial leverage. AI agents do the coordination work, senior operators handle judgment, and partners do the physical work.
2. A transparent network where everyone who adds value to the chain (customs brokers, compliance experts, 3PLs, marketing agencies, financiers) earns in proportion to what they contribute, and everyone else can verify what was done and what is owed.

## How we avoid becoming the middleman

If Vybd succeeds, Vybd becomes the middleman. That is unavoidable, so the question is what kind. Our commitments:

- The producer owns their customer. Customer data, storefronts and channel accounts sit in the brand's name, not ours.
- Fees are visible. Every brand sees what Vybd and each partner charged on every shipment and order.
- Leaving is easy. A brand can take its data, playbooks and partner relationships with it.
- Value is verifiable. Partners see the work recorded against them and what they're owed; brands see what they paid for.

## The plan: three stages

We get from today to a network where any producer reaches consumers directly in three stages. Each one earns the assets the next needs (flow, then data, then trust, then capital), and we move on only when a gate is met.

1. Operator (now). We run US market entry end to end for brands from India, South Korea and China. The fees matter, but the real outputs are codified playbooks and agents, a partner bench scored on performance, and proprietary data on landed costs, compliance and sell-through. Gate: operator hours per brand fall every quarter, and days to first sale fall with them.
2. Orchestrator. Partners receive work through Vybd, and Vybd becomes the system of record for shipments, invoices and payouts between brands and partners. The transparent ledger starts here as an internal tool: every partner sees the work done and the money owed. Gate: most partner work in our lane runs on our rails, and partners ask to join because we send them business.
3. Network and capital. We open the ledger so outside partners can plug in, and use our operating data to finance inventory and freight. That is where we take on the financial leverage incumbents hold. Selective brand ownership fits here, as the highest-conviction use of that data.

## What I've learned

When I started, I thought the problem was that middlemen existed. Now I think the problem is that intermediation is expensive, opaque and controlled by whoever holds the capital or the customer. AI can make coordination nearly free. Volume can make partners accountable. Data can make capital cheaper. Put together, that is how a producer anywhere reaches a consumer anywhere without giving most of the value away.

I still believe the goal I timidly gave in that interview. I just know now that you don't get there by declaring the middlemen gone. You get there by doing their job better, cheaper and in the open, one lane at a time.
