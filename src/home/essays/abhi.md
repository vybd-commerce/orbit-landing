For most of my career, I have been a supply chain operator. I did not come from artificial intelligence or software engineering, nor was I looking for the next technology trend. My career was built around the practical realities of running businesses: inventory, forecasting, suppliers, warehouses, freight, fulfillment, working capital, ERP systems and the constant challenge of getting the right product to the right place at the right cost.

Over the past decade, I have had the opportunity to experience those challenges in very different environments.

At The Body Shop, I worked within a large North American retail and wholesale network supporting hundreds of stores and major retail partners. At Infarm, supply chain meant something entirely different: sourcing and deploying complex modular farming infrastructure in a rapidly evolving business. Later, at Full Circle Brands, I led global supply chain operations for a consumer-products business of approximately $100 million in revenue, with roughly $13 million in supply-chain spend.

The companies, products and operating models changed, but many of the underlying problems did not.

Over those years, supply-chain technology improved significantly. I worked with SAP, NetSuite, MRP systems, forecasting applications, Tableau and, of course, Excel. These tools gave organizations better data, stronger controls and greater visibility. Yet I increasingly noticed a disconnect between the sophistication of the technology we were buying and how much manual work remained necessary to actually run the business.

A planner might have access to sales history, inventory, forecasts and open purchase orders, but turning that information into a decision still required considerable effort. Data had to be reconciled, exceptions investigated, suppliers contacted and recommendations translated into action. Even sophisticated organizations often depended on Excel and institutional knowledge to connect systems that were technically already integrated.

We had become very good at digitizing transactions. We were much less successful at digitizing decisions.

That distinction ultimately changed the direction of my career.

## The Excel Formula That Changed How I Thought About Software

My introduction to generative AI was surprisingly ordinary: I needed help with Excel.

For years, when I needed an unfamiliar formula or solution, my process was similar to everyone else's. Search Google, find an Excel forum or Reddit discussion, compare several answers, modify somebody else's formula and test it until it worked.

Then I started using ChatGPT.

Instead of searching for someone who had previously encountered a problem similar to mine, I could describe the actual problem I was trying to solve. I didn't necessarily need to know the function, terminology or even the correct way to structure the question.

Initially, I treated the answers cautiously. I tested them extensively and back-tested results against work I had already completed. The technology wasn't always right, but something important had changed: the distance between a business question and a usable answer had become dramatically shorter.

The significance went well beyond Excel.

For most of my career, enterprise software required the user to understand the system. You learned its menus, reports, transaction codes and terminology. If you wanted an answer that crossed multiple systems, you often exported the information and combined it somewhere else.

Generative AI began reversing that relationship. Instead of humans constantly learning how to communicate with software, software was beginning to understand what humans were trying to accomplish.

For a supply-chain operator, that raised a fascinating question: If I can explain a complicated problem to ChatGPT in plain English, why can't I interact with my supply-chain systems the same way?

Consider replenishment. A planner trying to determine what to purchase needs to consider demand, current inventory, open orders, incoming POs, supplier lead times, safety stock, MOQs and potentially several other variables. The calculations themselves are not necessarily difficult. The challenge is assembling the information, interpreting it and converting the conclusion into action.

What if AI could continuously perform that analysis, identify the exceptions that genuinely required human judgment and initiate the routine actions automatically?

That was when I stopped thinking about AI primarily as a better way to find information. I began thinking about it as a potential operating layer for a business.

That realization played an important role in my decision to join Vybd AI.

From the outside, moving from senior supply-chain leadership to an AI company might look like a career change. I saw it differently.

I didn't leave supply chain. I went looking for a better way to run one.

By then, I understood the gap between how supply-chain processes were designed and how they operated in the real world. I had watched capable planners spend valuable time reconciling data rather than planning, while executives with sophisticated technology stacks still struggled to get timely answers to relatively straightforward business questions. Forecasting combined models with judgment and context, while Excel and institutional knowledge frequently remained the invisible layer connecting everything.

That experience influenced how I approached Vybd. Rather than starting with the question, "Where can we add AI?", we could start with a much more practical one:

Why does a person still have to do this?

That question eventually became particularly important with one of our companies, Bayangrom.

## Bayangrom: Putting the Theory to Work

Bayangrom is an apparel company generating approximately $5 million in revenue, with more than 80 SKUs, two warehouses and sales across Amazon, DTC and TikTok Shop.

It was also a growing company operating in a market where future demand was difficult to predict. Forecasting was challenging, inventory levels were high, and significant parts of order processing and fulfillment still depended on manual activity.

For us, Bayangrom became an opportunity to test how far AI-enabled automation could realistically go inside a functioning consumer business.

We did not want to build another dashboard.

Traditional systems are already good at recording and displaying what has happened—sales, inventory, orders, forecasts, purchasing and fulfillment performance. The harder part of operating a business begins after that information becomes available: determining what it means, what deserves attention and what should happen next.

At Bayangrom, we therefore approached the problem as an operating workflow rather than simply a reporting project.

We brought together information across sales channels, inventory, purchasing, suppliers, orders and fulfillment. We applied AI and automation to forecasting, inventory planning, replenishment, purchase-order processes, supplier follow-up, order processing and fulfillment workflows. Where human oversight remained valuable, dashboards and exception management provided visibility without requiring people to manually reconstruct the underlying information.

The objective was not automation for its own sake. It was to allow the system to handle predictable work while directing human attention toward situations where experience and judgment actually mattered.

The results validated much of what had originally attracted me to AI.

Bayangrom achieved approximately an 88% reduction in manual workload and a 12% reduction in fulfillment costs.

Those two numbers matter together.

An 88% reduction in manual activity demonstrates what automation can do operationally. The 12% reduction in fulfillment costs demonstrates why a CEO should care financially.

For me, Bayangrom was the point where AI-enabled supply chain moved from an interesting thesis to something tangible.

## The Opportunity Is Not to Replace Your ERP

One misconception about AI transformation is that companies need to replace the technology they already have.

I don't believe they do.

ERP, WMS, OMS and e-commerce platforms serve essential functions and remain valuable systems of record and execution. Companies have invested years implementing them, and replacing everything with an "AI platform" would often create more problems than it solves.

The opportunity is to introduce intelligence across those systems.

Imagine a layer capable of understanding sales activity, inventory positions, incoming supply, forecasts and operational constraints simultaneously. Rather than requiring an employee to repeatedly interrogate those systems, reconcile their outputs and determine what deserves attention, AI can continuously monitor the environment and surface the decisions that matter.

That changes the role of technology.

A traditional dashboard might tell a planner that inventory has fallen.

An intelligent operating layer should help determine whether the decline represents a genuine risk, consider what inventory is already inbound, account for demand and supplier lead time, recommend an appropriate response and potentially prepare the action for approval.

The distinction is subtle but important.

It is the difference between showing someone a problem and helping solve it.

## What This Means for People

The experience also changed how I think about the debate around AI replacing jobs.

Supply-chain organizations employ talented people because of their judgment, commercial understanding and ability to navigate uncertainty. Yet we routinely consume a significant portion of their time with work that does not require those abilities.

A demand planner's greatest value is not manipulating spreadsheets. A procurement professional's greatest value is not repeatedly emailing suppliers for updates. And a supply-chain leader should not spend valuable time assembling reports simply to understand what happened yesterday.

AI creates an opportunity to shift that balance.

At Bayangrom, reducing manual workload by approximately 88% did not mean eliminating 88% of the value people provided. It meant removing much of the repetitive work standing between those people and the higher-value decisions we actually needed them to make.

That is an important distinction for any CEO considering AI.

## The Question I Would Ask as a CEO

If I were running a $5 million, $20 million or $100 million product company today, I would not begin by asking whether my company needs an AI strategy.

I would start with the operation itself.

I would identify where talented employees spend time gathering information rather than acting on it; where reports are downloaded and reconciled manually; where orders are repeatedly entered or checked; where routine replenishment decisions consume planner capacity; and where employees are effectively functioning as the integration layer between systems.

Then I would ask a much more consequential question:

What would happen to the economics and scalability of this business if most of that work disappeared?

Could the company grow without adding overhead at the same rate? Could we carry less inventory? Improve service levels? React to demand changes faster? Reduce fulfillment costs? Give executives answers in minutes rather than waiting for someone to build another report?

Those are not really AI questions.

They are business questions.

Because once you see how much of the work can be automated, the more interesting question becomes why you are still doing it manually.
